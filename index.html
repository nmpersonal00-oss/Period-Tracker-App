01. The body & the science

What happens inside the body, why bleeding occurs, and why the timing can change.

What is a period, and why does it happen?

A menstrual period is the shedding of the uterine lining (endometrium). Blood and tissue pass through the cervix and vagina. This is a normal biological process, not an injury or the body being “unclean.” The cycle prepares the uterus for a possible pregnancy; when pregnancy does not occur, estrogen and progesterone drop and the lining is released.

Cycle day 1 means the first day of menstrual bleeding, not the day it stops. Adult cycles commonly fall around 21–35 days; bleeding often lasts about 2–7 days. A single 28-day example is not a rule for everyone.

Uterus, ovaries, cervix and vagina: who does what?

Ovaries contain developing eggs and produce important hormones. Fallopian tubes are where an egg may travel after ovulation. The uterus grows and sheds its inner lining in response to hormonal signals. The cervix is the lower opening of the uterus. The vagina is the passage through which menstrual flow leaves the body.

Period blood comes from the uterus; it is not the same as urine. Most menstrual flow is blood mixed with shed uterine tissue and fluid.

The four phases, explained carefully

Menstruation: Bleeding begins as hormone levels are low and the lining sheds. Tiredness or cramps may occur, but some people feel well.

Follicular phase: Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) helps follicles develop. Estrogen generally rises, and the uterine lining begins rebuilding. This phase can vary considerably in length.

Ovulation: A luteinizing hormone (LH) surge triggers release of an egg. Ovulation does not always happen on day 14, and timing varies from cycle to cycle. Calendar guesses cannot confirm it.

Luteal phase: Progesterone rises after ovulation and helps maintain the uterine lining. Without a pregnancy, hormone levels fall, which can bring premenstrual symptoms and then bleeding. These phases overlap; they are not rigid emotional personality types.

The hormones: estrogen, progesterone, FSH and LH

FSH supports follicle growth; LH is involved in triggering ovulation; estrogen contributes to growth of the uterine lining; and progesterone helps prepare and maintain the lining after ovulation. These hormones communicate in feedback loops between the brain, pituitary gland, and ovaries.

Hormonal changes can affect sleep, temperature, energy, appetite, and sensitivity to symptoms, but no single mood or behavior can be assumed from a cycle phase. Stress, illness, medication, sleep, and many other factors matter too.

Fertility, pregnancy, and what this app cannot know

Pregnancy is possible when sperm are present around the time an egg is released. The fertile window is related to sperm and egg survival, but varies because ovulation timing varies. A missed period may have several causes, including pregnancy.

Blossom's blue-star ovulation marker is only a calendar estimate. It cannot verify ovulation and must not be relied on to prevent pregnancy. Decisions about contraception, fertility awareness, or pregnancy testing should be based on appropriate medical advice and reliable methods—not this app's predicted dates.

02. Symptoms & what they mean

Taking discomfort seriously without assuming every period feels the same.

Why cramps hurt: prostaglandins and contractions

During menstruation, the uterus contracts to help release its lining. Chemical messengers called prostaglandins contribute to these contractions and can make them painful. Pain may spread to the back or thighs and can occur with nausea or bowel changes.

Some people benefit from warmth or gentle movement. Standard period advice often recommends NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or naproxen, but these may be unsafe for people with one kidney or other medical risks and should not be assumed appropriate. Severe pain that disrupts daily life deserves evaluation, even if it happens every month. The specialized chapter below explains this in more detail.

Bleeding, fatigue, headaches and digestive changes

Flow can vary by day and cycle. Some people have tender breasts, bloating, headaches, nausea, diarrhea, constipation, fatigue or lower-back discomfort. Heavier bleeding can contribute to iron deficiency or anemia, which may bring weakness, breathlessness or dizziness.

Helpful support can be as concrete as filling a water bottle, providing a heating pad, cooking a meal, keeping products available, or taking over physically demanding chores. Ask about comfort preferences; some people prefer quiet, others company.

PMS, PMDD and emotions: no stereotypes

PMS means physical and/or emotional symptoms that tend to appear after ovulation and before bleeding, sometimes including irritability, fatigue, sleep problems, anxiety, tenderness and cravings. PMDD is a more severe, impairing pattern of premenstrual mood symptoms that needs clinical attention.

Not every woman has PMS, and not every conflict is hormone-driven. A partner should listen to her words, not dismiss concerns as “just her period.” If mood symptoms become intense, frightening, or disrupt daily life, professional support is appropriate.

Products, hygiene and comfort — what a partner should know

Common options include pads, period underwear, tampons, and menstrual cups or discs. Pads and underwear collect blood outside the body; tampons absorb it internally; cups and discs collect it internally. The best choice is personal and may depend on comfort, health, activities, accessibility, and religious preferences.

Wash hands before and after handling products and follow each product's instructions. The FDA advises changing a tampon every 4–8 hours, never longer than eight hours, and using the lowest absorbency needed. Menstrual products should be changed and cleaned as directed. Severe sudden fever, vomiting, faintness or unusual illness while using a tampon requires urgent medical attention.

He can help by remembering the specific products she likes, keeping a discrete supply ready, and never criticizing the choice she makes.

Why cycles change and what to track

Cycle length and symptoms can shift with age, stress, sleep, illness, medications, changes in weight or exercise, hormonal contraception, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and other health conditions. That does not mean every irregularity is serious, but persistent changes deserve attention.

Blossom records dates and symptoms so patterns can be noticed over time. It does not diagnose conditions. Helpful details to bring to an appointment include bleeding days, severity, pain location, symptoms, medications tried, and how work or daily life is affected.

03. How to care for her

Physical, mental and emotional support grounded in listening and consent.

Physical care: small acts that matter
  • Ask whether heat on the lower abdomen or back would feel soothing, and check it is not too hot.
  • Offer water, a meal, comfortable clothes, menstrual products, and a calm place to rest.
  • Take over shopping, cleaning, meals or other tasks without making her manage every detail.
  • Offer gentle stretching, a short walk or massage only if she wants it.
  • Help make a medical appointment if symptoms are severe or changing, without pressuring her.

No single intervention works for everyone. Her comfort and medical history matter more than generic advice.

Mental care: reduce the number of decisions

Fatigue, pain and poor sleep can make small tasks feel unusually demanding. Make choices easier: offer two meal options rather than ten, protect quiet time, handle routine chores, and remember supplies without a long conversation about them.

If she is sensitive to light, noise, movement or screens, reduce stimulation. A gentle voice, dimmer room, less clutter, and the option to postpone nonurgent conversations can be especially supportive.

Emotional care: listen without minimizing

Try: “Would you like comfort, practical help, or just someone to listen?” Validate her experience without guessing the cause. Ask how she's doing rather than deciding what she must be feeling from an app prediction.

Avoid: “You're overreacting,” “It's just hormones,” or expecting her to explain her discomfort repeatedly. Being kind doesn't mean avoiding normal communication; it means choosing a respectful moment and making room for both people's feelings.

Why sensory sensitivity and dizziness deserve attention

Pain, fatigue, poor sleep, migraine, nausea, and stress can make bright lights, strong smells, rapid motion, or noise feel worse. This does not happen to everyone and is not necessarily caused by the menstrual cycle; it is a reason to ask about her needs rather than guess.

Lower screen brightness, reduce unnecessary notifications, offer a quieter environment, avoid strong fragrances, and let her rest or step away from overwhelming places. If dizziness is frequent, severe, associated with heavy bleeding, or occurs with fainting, it deserves medical assessment.

What a thoughtful day of support can look like

Morning: Ask how she slept and whether she wants breakfast, warmth, or a quieter start. Afternoon: Take responsibility for a concrete task and keep plans flexible. Evening: Check whether symptoms have changed, offer comfort without expecting it to be accepted, and protect uninterrupted sleep when possible.

The goal is not for him to manage her body or schedule, but to share ordinary responsibilities so she can choose what helps. A day without symptoms should not lead to an assumption that support is no longer needed.

Care and intimacy: mutual choice, never pressure

Periods can affect desire, tenderness, energy and comfort in different ways. Affection might mean holding hands, sitting together, a shoulder rub, or giving her space. Ask what kind of touch she wants; consent matters every day and can change at any time.

Menstrual-cycle tracking cannot determine whether sexual activity is safe from pregnancy or infection. Follow your own beliefs and health guidance for intimacy, and discuss boundaries privately and respectfully.

When anxiety, depression or conflict seem cycle-related

Track the timing of distress for a few cycles without using it as a label. Symptoms that reliably intensify before menstruation, ease after bleeding begins, and interfere with life may be worth discussing with a clinician. Existing anxiety, depression, migraines or other medical conditions can also worsen before a period.

He can offer to accompany her to an appointment or help write down concerns if she wants. If either partner is in immediate danger or there are thoughts of self-harm, seek urgent help; don't rely on an app or a relationship conversation alone. Emotional care includes supporting professional treatment when needed.

Conversation starters for the two of you
  • “What usually helps most when your cramps begin?”
  • “Would you like me to take care of dinner and cleaning today?”
  • “Is the room too bright or noisy? I can change that.”
  • “Would you prefer quiet company, closeness, or time alone?”
  • “Is there anything from last month that we can do better this time?”

A five-minute calm check-in can be more useful than a long lecture.

04. Our relationship & spiritual care

Sharing responsibilities, protecting dignity, and honoring faith without making medical or religious assumptions.

A shared care plan, not a surveillance tool

Decide together whether he should see reminders, estimates or shared notes. Health tracking belongs to the person experiencing it. A shared account should never mean her symptoms or fertility data become open to demands, monitoring, or judgment.

Choose simple responsibilities: keep supplies replenished, notice when rest helps, plan flexible dates, and review recurring difficulties together. Celebrate that you are a team rather than rating her mood or her cycle.

Faith, patience and meaningful spiritual support

Ask what spiritual support is comforting: prayer or du'a, a quiet place for reflection, listening to recitation, gentle words, helping with household duties, or simply being present. Follow the religious practices and trusted guidance that you both choose.

If you observe Islamic practices, menstrual and purity questions—including prayer, fasting, ghusl and intimacy—may require particular rulings that differ by school and circumstance. This guide is not a fatwa. Blossom's existing Ibadah journal remains a personal log; it does not decide purity or religious obligations for you.

A gentle monthly conversation you can repeat

Once a month, choose an unhurried moment and discuss three questions: What felt most difficult? What support actually helped? What would we like to do differently? Listen without correcting memory or minimizing symptoms. He can also share his own needs with kindness.

Write down practical agreements only if you both want to. You can choose not to share sensitive symptom notes in the app. Care should feel like partnership, not assessment or performance.

Support when the period is not the whole story

A person can have an emotionally hard day unrelated to the menstrual cycle. Relationship stress, life events, anxiety, grief, chronic illness, and everyday tiredness deserve the same empathy.

When disagreements arise, avoid using cycle data as evidence against her. Ask whether she wants to revisit the conversation after resting; return to it respectfully instead of ignoring it forever.

05. Health, safety & getting help

A practical reference for when kindness includes seeking care.

When to seek medical advice

Contact a healthcare professional about bleeding lasting longer than about seven days, repeated soaking of pads or tampons every one to two hours, bleeding between periods or after sex, new or worsening severe pain, persistent irregular cycles, or symptoms that disrupt daily activities. It is reasonable to seek care sooner whenever something feels concerning.

Seek urgent assessment for heavy bleeding with faintness, marked dizziness, racing heartbeat, severe unusual pelvic pain, or other concerning symptoms. If pregnancy is possible and there is severe pain or heavy bleeding, seek urgent medical advice.

Conditions that can cause difficult periods

Severe pain, abnormal bleeding, or significant premenstrual mood symptoms may be associated with treatable conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, hormonal or thyroid disorders, bleeding disorders, or PMDD. A clinician may use symptom history, examination, blood tests, imaging or other evaluation as appropriate.

No app can diagnose these conditions. Early medical conversations can be useful even when symptoms seem familiar.

Myths and better truths
  • Myth: Every cycle is exactly 28 days. Reality: There is meaningful natural variation.
  • Myth: Period pain should always be endured. Reality: Help and effective treatments exist.
  • Myth: Mood changes explain every emotion. Reality: Listen to the person, not a stereotype.
  • Myth: A calendar predicts a safe day to avoid pregnancy. Reality: Calendar estimates alone are not reliable contraception.
  • Myth: Menstrual blood is dirty or shameful. Reality: Menstruation is a normal biological process.
How to prepare for a useful health appointment

Bring the timeline of period starts and ends, symptoms, pain severity and location, bleeding intensity, any intermenstrual spotting, daily-life impact, and treatments already tried. She decides what private information to share and who attends the appointment.

This guide is general educational information, not individual medical advice. If either of you has a concern about symptoms, ask a licensed clinician.

06. When the pain is more than a period

Vascular EDS, severe menstrual pain, protecting one kidney, and being a steady partner when ordinary pain-relief advice does not fit.

What he should understand first

Her pain can be intense, disabling and frightening. She does not have to prove how much it hurts. With vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (vEDS) and a single kidney, it is especially important to have an individualized plan from her clinicians. Not every pain is “just cramps,” and not every standard pain medicine is an appropriate option.

Vascular EDS: what it is and why it matters

Vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (vEDS) is a rare inherited connective-tissue condition, usually related to changes in the COL3A1 gene. Type III collagen helps support blood vessels and certain organs. In vEDS, the walls of arteries, the bowel and sometimes the uterus can be unusually fragile. It is not the same diagnosis as hypermobile EDS (hEDS), and the risks should not be treated as interchangeable.

vEDS does not mean that every period will cause vascular injury, or that an emergency is inevitable. It does mean unexplained, sudden or markedly different pain deserves a much lower threshold for urgent assessment. He should know her diagnosis details, emergency contact plan and where her clinicians recommend going for urgent care.

Good support: ask her what her usual cramps feel like, notice when a new episode is different, and tell medical staff about confirmed or suspected vEDS when seeking care. Do not attempt to diagnose a rupture at home.

How vEDS relates to menstruation — and what science does not know

Menstrual hormones and uterine contractions operate in someone with vEDS much as they do in other people. Prostaglandins can still trigger very painful uterine contractions. Separately, vEDS affects connective-tissue integrity. Studies of people with EDS more broadly report menstrual pain and heavy bleeding, but many studies combine different EDS types or rely on self-reported diagnoses. Those findings should not be taken as proof that vEDS itself causes a particular person's severe cramps.

Severe or worsening period pain may also come from another gynecologic condition, such as endometriosis, adenomyosis or fibroids, or from a non-gynecologic problem. Her pain deserves an individualized assessment rather than being attributed automatically to EDS or menstruation.

Why cramps can feel unbearable: the pain pathways

When progesterone falls before a period, the uterine lining releases prostaglandins. They can intensify contractions and temporarily reduce oxygen delivery to uterine muscle, contributing to cramping. Pain can spread to the lower back and thighs and arrive with nausea, diarrhea, exhaustion, or a headache. Poor sleep and repeated painful episodes can increase distress and the burden of symptoms; that does not make the pain imaginary.

Primary dysmenorrhea is pain without another identified pelvic disease. Secondary dysmenorrhea is pain related to a condition or other medical cause. Disabling or worsening pain, pain outside bleeding days, bleeding between periods, or pain with bowel movements or urination are reasons to discuss a targeted evaluation with a gynecologist. Endometriosis is one condition to consider, but not the only one.

He can write down her exact words about pain location, intensity, duration and whether this episode is her usual pattern, if she wants him to. That record can help clinicians distinguish recurring menstrual pain from something new.

One kidney: why common period painkillers may not be safe

Many period-pain websites recommend NSAIDs such as ibuprofen (Advil/Motrin), naproxen (Aleve) or aspirin. NSAIDs can reduce blood flow through the kidneys and sometimes cause acute kidney injury, especially when a person is dehydrated or already has kidney problems. Guidance for people living with one kidney advises avoiding over-the-counter NSAIDs unless their treating clinician has specifically advised otherwise.

Having one kidney does not automatically mean kidney failure or that all pain medicines are forbidden. Safety depends on kidney function, medical history, other medicines and the cause of pain. Do not offer her an over-the-counter painkiller, a new supplement or someone else's prescription without checking her individualized medical instructions.

Acetaminophen (paracetamol/Tylenol) is generally considered less harmful to kidneys at recommended doses, but it has its own risks and limitations and may not help severe cramps. It should be discussed with her clinician, particularly given her medical history. The goal is a written plan from her gynecologist and kidney care team, not trial-and-error advice from an app.

Support during severe pain when medication is limited

First ask: “Is this your familiar period pain, or something different?” If the pain is sudden, unusual, escalating, or accompanied by concerning symptoms, focus on medical evaluation rather than at-home treatment.

For her familiar cramps, and only if she finds them comfortable, gentle non-drug comfort measures may include a warm (not scalding) compress with a fabric barrier, rest in a supported position, soft clothing, a quiet dim room, a warm shower, or slow breathing. Some people benefit from gentle movement or a clinically approved TENS device. Avoid vigorous abdominal manipulation, aggressive massage, chiropractic adjustments or strenuous activity without guidance from her vEDS team.

He can bring supplies, reduce noise, prepare food, take over chores and keep her phone and medical information nearby. Follow her kidney specialist's advice about fluids—do not force fluids if she has medical restrictions. These supports can improve comfort but are not a substitute for assessing severe or unusual pain.

When “period pain” needs urgent or emergency care

With known or suspected vEDS, sudden, severe, unfamiliar or rapidly worsening pain in the abdomen, pelvis, flank, back, chest or head needs emergency medical assessment. Arterial or organ complications can initially look like other causes of pain; only an appropriate medical evaluation can distinguish them. He should tell the emergency team about vEDS and any emergency letter from her specialist.

Also seek emergency care for fainting, collapse, severe weakness, trouble breathing, new neurologic symptoms, heavy bleeding with dizziness, or severe pelvic pain when pregnancy is possible. Fever with significant pelvic pain, inability to urinate, or pain that is distinctly worse than normal also warrants urgent assessment. He should not tell her to “wait for the cramps to pass” when any of these occur.

Before an emergency: agree on who to call, where to go, what documentation to bring, and how to describe her typical pain versus a new symptom. If she is acutely unwell, call local emergency services rather than driving her yourself.

What clinicians might investigate or discuss

A gynecologist may review bleeding patterns, pain severity and location, symptoms between periods, anemia risk, and possible causes such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids or pelvic-floor pain. Blood tests and imaging may be considered according to symptoms. When vEDS is part of the picture, her team should weigh the risks and benefits of any invasive procedure and coordinate with clinicians familiar with tissue fragility.

Hormonal approaches can sometimes reduce bleeding or menstrual pain, but choice of treatment—including combined hormonal methods, progestin-only methods and an intrauterine device—requires individualized review of vascular risks, blood-pressure status, kidney function, personal preferences, and procedural considerations. This guide does not identify any hormonal method as universally safe or unsafe for her.

Ask for a practical plan covering ordinary cramps, medication restrictions, rescue options for severe episodes, signs that mean “go now,” and which specialist to contact when a treatment is ineffective.

Protecting the remaining kidney in everyday care

A person may live well with one kidney, but that kidney deserves regular follow-up. Her clinician may monitor blood pressure, blood or urine markers of kidney function and protein in the urine. He can help keep appointments and medication lists organized only if she wants that help.

NSAIDs sometimes appear unexpectedly in combination cold remedies, headache products, or prescription medicines. A pharmacist can help check ingredients. During heavy bleeding, vomiting, diarrhea, or illness, dehydration can raise kidney-injury risk and may warrant early medical advice. Follow the hydration plan from her clinician rather than guessing at a target.

Do not interpret one kidney as a reason to restrict food, fluid or activity without instructions. The safest approach is to know her own lab results and care plan.

If pregnancy or family planning is discussed

Pregnancy carries important additional vascular and uterine risks in vEDS. If pregnancy is possible, suspected, or being considered, preconception guidance from a high-risk maternal-fetal medicine team familiar with vEDS and her other specialists is important. Severe pain with possible pregnancy always needs timely assessment.

The app's predicted ovulation dates are only estimates—not confirmation of fertility and not contraception. He should respect that discussions about reproduction can be sensitive; decisions belong to the people involved and their clinical team.

How to be her partner when pain takes over

Believe her immediately. Severe pain can make conversation, light, sound and decisions overwhelming. Use short questions: “Do you want heat?” “Would you prefer quiet company?” “Is this your usual pain?” Then respect the answer. Taking care of meals, chores, phone calls or transport can be more helpful than repeatedly asking what to do.

If she cannot tolerate bright screens or noise, dim lights, silence unnecessary notifications and keep this app out of her sight unless she asks for it. Keep a calm record of symptoms if she consents. If she feels scared, offer reassurance without minimizing the seriousness: “I'm here. We can follow the plan together.”

Protect dignity and boundaries. Pain, emotional strain, intimacy preferences and spiritual practices are individual—not signs of weakness. What helps one month may not help the next.

Build a written personal pain-and-safety plan together

At a nonurgent appointment, ask her clinicians to help write a plan she can actually use:

  • What sensations are typical for her period, and what new symptoms need urgent evaluation?
  • With her one kidney, which medicines or products should she avoid, and which—if any—are approved at what dose?
  • What are the non-drug steps she prefers and any activities or devices her vascular team discourages?
  • Should she be assessed for endometriosis, adenomyosis, anemia or another reason for debilitating pain?
  • Who should he contact when she is vomiting, faint, unable to function, or experiencing pain outside her usual pattern?
  • Should she carry a vEDS emergency letter and kidney-related medication list?

Write the answers in a place she controls. This app is for understanding and support; it cannot replace her specialists' advice.
